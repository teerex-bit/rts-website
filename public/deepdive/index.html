AN INVITATION TO LIFE WITH GOD

A Different Way of Living With God

What if the Christian life was never intended to be primarily about what we accomplish for God, but about what becomes possible when we learn to live with Him?

Reforming the Soul is an invitation to explore that possibility.

Throughout Scripture, we discover a God who desires more than our obedience, service, or religious devotion. He desires relationship. From the beginning, humanity was created for life in His presence—not merely to believe in Him, but to walk with Him.

And that relationship was never intended to be separate from ordinary life.

This journey explores what it means to recover that way of living. Not through another collection of religious obligations, but through a process of spiritual formation that reaches beyond what we know and into who we are becoming.

Along the way, we will learn to recognize what has shaped us, see ourselves and God more clearly, live with Him in the present, and participate in what He is doing in the world around us.

There is no spiritual performance to achieve here, and no ideal version of yourself you must become before beginning.

The invitation is simply to discover what life with God can actually become.

The Person We've Become

Most of us begin the journey of spiritual formation already deeply formed. Long before we intentionally thought about who we were becoming, life was shaping us. Our families shaped us. Relationships shaped us. Success and failure shaped us. Pain, disappointment, fear, culture, church, and our experiences with other people all contributed to the people we became.

Much of that formation happened without our awareness.

We learned how to protect ourselves before we knew we were protecting ourselves. We developed ways of responding to conflict, disappointment, rejection, and uncertainty. We formed expectations about what other people would do and what we needed to do in response. We learned what made us feel safe and what made us feel threatened. We discovered ways to gain approval, avoid pain, maintain control, or keep people at a comfortable distance.

Eventually, these responses became so familiar that they simply felt like us.

Then we came to God carrying all of it.

We may have learned what Christians believe and how Christians are supposed to behave. We may have learned Scripture, attended church, prayed, served, worshiped, and genuinely desired to follow Jesus. Yet underneath all of that, the person who had been formed through years of living was still there.

That is where Reforming the Soul begins.

It does not begin by asking us to try harder to behave like better Christians. It begins by helping us recognize the person from whom our behavior is already coming.

A MOMENT TO NOTICE

What has shaped you?

Much of our formation happened without our awareness. Tap an experience to explore how it can shape us. Notice what resonates with your own story; there is no right answer.

Our earliest surroundings often teach us what feels normal, safe, and expected. We may carry those lessons into adulthood without realizing where they began.

Relationships teach us what closeness, trust, acceptance, and rejection feel like. Over time, those experiences can shape what we expect from others, how much of ourselves we reveal, and how we respond when connection feels uncertain.

Success can teach us to associate worth with achievement. Failure can make us hesitant to try again. Neither experience has to define us, but both can influence what we believe about ourselves.

Pain can leave us expecting another loss, protecting ourselves before anything has happened. Sometimes a response that once helped us endure a difficult season continues long after that season has passed.

When approval or belonging seems tied to meeting someone else’s expectations, we may learn to perform, please, or hide parts of ourselves. Those habits can feel like personality rather than something we learned.

Not every formative experience fits a category. A particular season, responsibility, change, or unexpected moment may have influenced the way you see yourself, others, or God.

You may explore as many as you like, or continue without choosing.

AWAKEN

We Begin by Waking Up

The first movement is Awaken, because we cannot intentionally surrender something we cannot see.

Imagine someone says something that makes you feel dismissed. Before you have had time to think about it, something happens inside you. Maybe you become defensive. Maybe you withdraw. Maybe you feel an immediate need to explain yourself. Perhaps you become angry or spend the rest of the day replaying the conversation.

Normally, we focus on the other person.

They shouldn't have said that.

Awaken asks us to notice something else.

What just happened in me?

That is a very different question.

We are not immediately deciding whether our response was right or wrong. We are not condemning ourselves for having it. We are simply learning to pay attention.

Why did that bother me so much? Why did I feel threatened? Why was it so important that I be understood? Why do I respond this way whenever someone questions me? Why does this particular situation keep producing the same reaction?

At first, we may recognize these things long after they happen. Then we begin catching them sooner. Eventually, we sometimes recognize them while they are happening.

That growing awareness is important because we are beginning to see the person beneath the behavior.

Much of what we call our personality may reflect ways we learned to navigate life. Our soul learned to protect us from rejection, disappointment, uncertainty, and pain. Those responses often served a purpose, but over time they could become automatic ways of interpreting and responding to the world.

We are not trying to eliminate the soul or condemn its protective responses. We are learning to bring the whole person into harmony with the Spirit, allowing God to become the source of security we once tried to manufacture for ourselves.

We are learning to catch ourselves being ourselves.

And eventually we can say, “Something is happening in me, and I can see it.”

That is awakening.

But seeing what is happening does not necessarily tell us why it is happening. For that, we have to look deeper.

JAMES 1:19–20 · WEB
“So, then, my beloved brothers, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger; for the anger of man doesn't produce the righteousness of God.”

James does not merely offer a rule for managing anger. He describes a different posture toward the moment: listening before reacting. The pause creates room to notice what is rising within us and to respond rather than simply repeat a familiar pattern.

AWAKEN · PRACTICING ATTENTION

Notice Without Needing to Explain

When something unsettles us, we usually turn our attention outward. We replay what someone said, what should have happened, or what needs to change. Sometimes those concerns are legitimate. But there is another part of the moment we can learn to notice: what is happening within us while we respond.

Jesus does not invite us into self-suspicion or endless analysis. He invites us into truth, and truth begins with the willingness to see. We can acknowledge a reaction without condemning ourselves for having it or rushing to justify it. Awareness is not yet surrender, but it gives us something real to bring into relationship with God.

PSALM 139:23–24 · WEB
“Search me, God, and know my heart. Try me, and know my thoughts. See if there is any wicked way in me, and lead me in the everlasting way.”

David's prayer is not an attempt to diagnose himself. He invites God to know what he cannot fully see and to lead him. The goal is not self-mastery. It is an honest, trusting relationship with the One who sees clearly.

An invitation, not an assessment

Think of a recent ordinary moment that stayed with you. Perhaps you felt dismissed, hurried, overlooked, praised, or unexpectedly interrupted. Instead of trying to uncover a hidden motive, simply remember what you noticed in yourself. You may have felt tension, urgency, a need to defend yourself, or a desire to withdraw. You may not be sure what you felt at all.

There is no score to earn, pattern to identify, or conclusion the app needs to produce. You can sit with the moment quietly, speak with God about it, or continue reading. The practice is learning to pay attention without needing to control what you discover.

“Lord, here is what I noticed. I do not need to understand it all before I bring it to You.”

PART I · SEE YOURSELF CLEARLY

Then We Learn to See Clearly

Our behavior does not come from nowhere. Beneath our reactions are beliefs, expectations, desires, fears, assumptions, and intentions. We do what we do because, at some level, our response makes sense to the person we have become.

This is why simply telling ourselves to behave differently often produces disappointing results. We may know that we should not be anxious and still experience anxiety. We may know that we should forgive and still carry resentment. We may know that God can be trusted while desperately trying to control an outcome.

The problem is not necessarily that we do not know the correct answer. Something deeper may be happening.

Awaken taught us to notice what is happening. See Clearly begins examining what is happening underneath—and what we actually believe to be true.

ROMANS 12:2 · WEB
“Don’t be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, so that you may prove what is the good, well-pleasing, and perfect will of God.”

Paul describes transformation as something deeper than modifying outward behavior. The mind must be renewed. That includes learning to recognize the interpretations and assumptions we have mistaken for truth. We are not simply collecting better ideas; we are learning to see life differently in God's presence.

When a reaction seems automatic, we can assume it reveals who we are. But a familiar response is not necessarily our identity. It may be something we learned to expect, protect, or repeat. The distinction matters: what has been formed within us can also be brought into the light and re-formed.

PART I · SEE YOURSELF CLEARLY · THE INNER PATH

What We See Shapes What We Become

What we see shapes what we believe. What we believe shapes what we expect. What we expect influences what we desire. What we desire influences what we intend.

An event happens, but I do not encounter it without interpretation. I notice certain details and give them meaning.

The meaning I give that event reinforces something I hold to be true about myself, others, or God.

What I believe shapes what I anticipate will happen next. I begin preparing for that expected outcome.

My expectations awaken a desire: to feel safe, be accepted, stay in control, or receive something I think I need.

That desire gives direction to my will. I begin forming a plan to secure what I want or avoid what I fear.

My intention becomes visible in a decision: what I say, what I withhold, how I respond, or what I do.

As choices are repeated, they become familiar patterns. What began as a way of seeing becomes a way of living.

PROVERBS 4:23 · WEB
“Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of life.”

The heart in biblical language is not merely a place for feelings. It includes the inner life from which our desires, judgments, and choices emerge. That is why the formation chain matters: behavior is often the visible expression of something already taking shape beneath the surface.

Consider a simple interruption. Someone changes a plan without asking you. The observable fact is that the plan changed. The meaning you assign might be that your needs do not matter. From there you may expect to be overlooked, desire to regain control, intend to confront or withdraw, and choose words that protect you. Yet another interpretation could lead to a different response. The point is not to insist that one explanation is correct, but to recognize that the event and the meaning you gave it are not identical.

Our intentions shape our choices, and our repeated choices eventually become the way we live. So Reforming the Soul begins looking underneath behavior, rather than merely trying to correct it.

Maybe what appears to be anger is actually self-protection. Perhaps what looks like ambition is being fueled by a need for approval. What looks like independence may actually reveal how difficult it is for us to trust someone else.

Changing an action is not always the same as changing the person from whom the action comes. We can restrain a response while continuing to hold the belief that produced it.

When a belief becomes an identity

We do not only form opinions about events. Over time we also form a story about ourselves: I am the responsible one. I am the one people leave. I have to be useful to be wanted. I must never make a mistake. A story repeated often enough can begin to feel like a fact, even when it was first learned in a particular season of life.

This is where performance, guilt, and self-protection can become intertwined. If my acceptance depends on being right, correction feels threatening. If my value depends on being needed, another person's independence may feel like rejection. If I have learned to expect disappointment, I may withdraw before anyone has had the opportunity to love me well. These are possible patterns, not labels we should assign to ourselves or others.

EPHESIANS 2:8–10 · WEB
“For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, that no one would boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works”

Paul puts belonging and God's workmanship before the good works that follow. Our actions matter, but they do not purchase our place with Him. Grace does not make faithful living irrelevant; it changes where faithful living comes from. We do not have to perform our way into an identity God gives us in Christ.

Guilt, shame, and the invitation to return

Guilt can direct our attention toward something we have done that needs to be acknowledged and repaired. Shame often moves further, persuading us that the failure tells the whole truth about who we are. One can lead toward confession and restoration; the other can tempt us to hide, defend, or despair. Scripture does not ask us to deny responsibility. It invites us to bring responsibility into the light of mercy rather than build our identity around failure.

In Genesis 3:8–10, the humans hide after their disobedience. Their actions have consequences, but hiding also changes how they approach God's presence. The question for formation is not only what went wrong. It is whether we will continue relating to God through concealment or learn to come into the light where truth and mercy can meet.

What did that moment seem to say about you, the other person, or God?

2 CORINTHIANS 10:5 · WEB
“bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ”

This phrase belongs to Paul's discussion of arguments and claims raised against the knowledge of God. It is not an invitation to obsess over every passing thought. Here it reminds us that what feels persuasive within us still needs to be brought into relationship with Christ and tested against what is true.

One helpful distinction is between what happened, what I concluded, and what I know. These may overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Sometimes clarity begins when we admit, “I know what happened, but I do not yet know what it meant.”

Independence can look like strength

Some of the habits we admire most can also keep us from receiving. Being capable, responsible, prepared, or dependable is not itself a problem. Yet when we believe we must always be sufficient for ourselves, dependence begins to feel like weakness. We can even bring that posture into prayer: asking God to bless what we have already decided rather than making room to listen.

JOHN 15:4–5 · WEB
“Remain in me, and I in you. As the branch can’t bear fruit by itself unless it remains in the vine, so neither can you, unless you remain in me.”

Jesus describes fruitfulness as the outcome of abiding, not independent achievement. A branch does not create life by trying harder to look alive. It receives life through connection. In the same way, formation is not a project in which we become self-sufficient Christians; it is learning to live from the life of Christ.

That is a deeper kind of clarity: recognizing not only what I do when I am afraid, but also the picture of life I have trusted. Am I trying to secure myself through control, approval, and performance, or learning to receive my life with God?

PART II · SEE GOD CLEARLY

SEE GOD CLEARLY

We have begun examining the person we have become. Now we turn toward the One we are learning to trust. What we believe about God shapes what we expect from Him, and those expectations influence how we live with Him.

What Do I Actually Believe About God?

Eventually, looking beneath our responses brings us to an even deeper question: What do I actually believe about God? Not simply what have I been taught about God, but what do I expect from Him when life is not going the way I want?

What do I expect from Him when I am disappointed? Do I believe He is good when He allows something I desperately wanted to avoid? Do I experience Him as a Father who wants me, or as someone constantly evaluating my performance?

Do I believe His acceptance must somehow be maintained through my behavior? Can I trust Him when I cannot understand what He is doing?

Knowing what Christians say about God is not necessarily the same as living from what we believe about Him.

These are not questions for judging ourselves. They help us recognize the expectations we actually bring into our relationship with God.

JOHN 14:9 · WEB
“He who has seen me has seen the Father.”

Jesus makes an extraordinary claim: the Father is not an unknown character behind Jesus whose disposition we must guess. If our inherited picture of God conflicts with the character Jesus reveals, that picture deserves to be examined. Jesus is not persuading a reluctant Father to love us; He is revealing the Father who sent Him.

Some of us can say that God loves us while living as though His welcome depends on how well we performed that day. Others believe He is sovereign but experience His authority primarily as control. The tension between what we confess and what we expect is not a reason for shame. It is a place where truth can begin to reshape relationship.

How our picture of God is formed

Our experience of authority often gives us an early vocabulary for understanding God. A distant parent, unpredictable leader, demanding religious environment, or season of unanswered prayer can influence what we anticipate from Him. Those experiences deserve honesty, but none is sufficient to define the Father. Even positive experiences can give us only a partial picture.

We might approach Him as disappointed before we have spoken, controlling before we have listened, or merely tolerating us until we improve. Such assumptions shape prayer and surrender. It is difficult to release control to someone we secretly believe is unkind. It is difficult to receive love while assuming every gift creates a debt.

LUKE 15:20 · WEB
“But while he was still far off, his father saw him, and was moved with compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.”

In Jesus' parable, the returning son's prepared speech does not create the father's compassion. The father's welcome meets him while he is still far away. The story does not erase the son's choices; it reveals a love that is already moving toward restoration. It challenges the picture of a father whose affection must first be earned back.

PART II · SEE GOD CLEARLY · JESUS AS OUR LENS

Learning to See God as He Is

This is the work of See Clearly. We begin examining what we believe about ourselves and what we believe about God. Jesus becomes the lens through which we learn to see the Father.

We may have learned to approach God as distant, disappointed, controlling, unpredictable, or merely tolerating us. Those expectations can shape how we pray, how we respond to difficulty, and whether we trust Him with what matters most.

Jesus does not simply give us another set of instructions. He reveals the Father's character. We can bring our inherited assumptions into the light of His life, His words, and the way He receives people.

Jesus is not the exception to what God is like. He is the revelation of what God is like.

Grace begins replacing our need to earn. Dependence begins replacing independence. Surrender begins challenging self-protection. We begin learning to trust the One we are actually coming to know.

HEBREWS 1:3 · WEB
“His Son is the radiance of his glory, the very image of his substance”

Jesus does more than offer information about God. His life makes the Father's character visible. We can therefore examine our assumptions in light of how Jesus responds to the fearful, the ashamed, the self-righteous, the overlooked, and those who fail. His compassion never requires us to pretend sin is harmless; His truth never requires us to imagine the Father is less loving than the Son.

To see God clearly is not to resolve every mystery about His providence or explain every disappointment. It is to know where to look when our circumstances seem to contradict His goodness. We return to Jesus, who reveals the Father's heart and invites us into trust before we can control the outcome.

Jesus reveals more than a reassuring idea

When Jesus touches those considered unclean, eats with people others reject, challenges religious performance, and forgives those who have failed, He is not temporarily suspending the Father's character. He is making that character known. His mercy is neither indifference to truth nor a reward for having already become worthy. He meets people in truth and calls them into life.

LUKE 10:22 · WEB
“No one knows who the Son is, except the Father, and who the Father is, except the Son, and he to whomever the Son desires to reveal him.”

Jesus invites us to know the Father He knows. We do not have to build our understanding of God from the authority figures we have experienced or the disappointments we have endured. We can bring those impressions to Jesus and allow what He reveals to reshape what we expect from the Father.

Jesus does not reveal His relationship with the Father merely as an example to admire. In John 14–17, He invites us into that same communion through the Spirit, sharing in the Father's love and learning to live from the attentiveness and dependence we see in Him.

Trust does not require an explanation for everything

Seeing God clearly does not mean every painful event becomes understandable. Scripture gives voice to lament, confusion, and waiting. The invitation is not to manufacture certainty about what God is doing. It is to learn the character of the One with whom we walk, so that unanswered questions do not have to become the final authority over our relationship with Him.

PART II · SEE GOD CLEARLY · WHAT IS TRUE

Understanding Where It Came From

Awaken made it possible to say, “I know what I did.” See Clearly invites us to go further: “I am starting to understand where that came from, and I am learning what is actually true.”

We may recognize that an angry response came from fear, that our need to control came from an expectation of abandonment, or that our desire for approval has shaped the way we relate to others. That recognition matters, but the goal is not to become experts at explaining ourselves.

We are learning to see ourselves and God truthfully, so that our relationship with Him can be lived from trust rather than fear, performance, or self-protection.

Trust precedes understanding.

But even clear understanding is not the same as becoming the kind of person who naturally lives from what is true.

From an inherited picture to a living relationship

There is a difference between agreeing that God is good and beginning to entrust a particular fear to Him. There is a difference between saying we are accepted in Christ and refusing to let failure drive us into hiding. In both cases, truth is moving from a statement we affirm toward a relationship we actually inhabit.

EPHESIANS 1:17–18 · WEB
“…that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give to you a spirit of wisdom and revelation in the knowledge of him, having the eyes of your hearts enlightened, that you may know what is the hope of his calling…”

Paul prays for people who already believe to see more clearly what God has given them. We can know the language of faith while still expecting rejection, disappointment, or distance from God. As He opens the eyes of our hearts, His welcome and the hope of His calling begin to shape how we understand ourselves and approach Him.

BECOME · LIVING WITH GOD

Learning to Live From Trust

We do not become different people merely by understanding ourselves more accurately. Our lives take shape in the small moments when something interrupts us, disappoints us, or asks more of us than we expected. These are not interruptions to formation. They are the places where what we believe becomes visible.

Jesus did not describe life with the Father as a collection of correct conclusions. He lived in attentive dependence, listening and responding within the circumstances before Him. We learn that way of life not by manufacturing extraordinary experiences but by remaining available to God in ordinary ones.

JOHN 5:19 · WEB
“Most certainly, I tell you, the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing. For whatever things he does, these the Son also does likewise.”

Jesus reveals a life of participation rather than independence. The invitation is not to imitate His authority, but to learn His posture of attentive relationship with the Father.

That kind of life takes practice. We learn to loosen our grip on outcomes, meet the present with God, respond faithfully, and return when we lose our way. The pattern is simple enough to remember, yet deep enough to reshape the way we live.

BECOME · RELEASE CONTROL

Release Control.

Release control

I release my demand that this situation must turn out the way I want before I can be okay.

Recognizing our need for control is not the same as surrendering it. We can understand the fear beneath our reactions and still demand that events unfold according to our expectations. Releasing control means giving up the claim that our preferred outcome must happen before we can trust God.

We can still name what we want, make responsible decisions, and act with courage. Surrender is not abandoning our will. It is choosing to bring our will into relationship with God's will, without insisting that we already know the best outcome.

Control can disguise itself as responsibility. We may call it careful planning, protecting someone, or making sure things go well. But when our peace depends on a particular outcome, responsibility has become a demand. We are trying to carry what belongs to God.

PROVERBS 3:5–6 · WEB
“Trust in Yahweh with all your heart, and don’t lean on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make your paths straight.”

Releasing control is not refusing to plan or neglecting what is entrusted to us. We still make decisions, tell the truth, set boundaries, and do what love requires. What we release is the insistence that our obedience must produce the result we prefer. We can tell God what we hope for and what we fear without demanding that He follow our timetable.

I am responsible for my response, not for controlling the outcome.

Trust precedes understanding. We can take the next faithful step even when we cannot see how the story will unfold.

Responsibility has a boundary. We are entrusted with decisions, honesty, effort, and love. We are not entrusted with guaranteeing another person's response or securing tomorrow's outcome. When we confuse the two, even good intentions can become a form of control. Surrender does not make us less responsible; it frees us to do what is ours without claiming what belongs to God.

BECOME · RECEIVE THE MOMENT

Receive the Moment.

Receive the moment

I can meet the life in front of me with God, rather than waiting for life to become what I wish it were.

After releasing our demand to control what happens, we become available to what is actually happening. So much of our attention lives in yesterday's regrets or tomorrow's possibilities. Yet the present is where we are able to listen, respond, and walk with God.

MATTHEW 6:34 · WEB
“Therefore don’t be anxious for tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Each day’s own evil is sufficient.”

Receiving the moment does not mean pretending that everything happening to us is good. It does not mean becoming passive or refusing to change something that should be changed. It means acknowledging that this is the moment in which I am presently living with God.

If God has allowed this moment to reach me, then this moment cannot prevent Him from forming me through it.

Receiving the moment also means paying attention to what it reveals. An interruption may uncover impatience, disappointment may expose our demand for certainty, and another person's words may reveal how deeply we seek approval. We do not need to hide those discoveries or immediately fix ourselves. We can bring them into our relationship with God.

As we become present to Him, the moment is no longer only a problem to solve. It becomes an opportunity to listen, to see ourselves truthfully, and to respond from trust instead of fear.

That changes the question.

Instead of asking only, How do I get out of this? I can begin asking, What does faithfulness look like here?

Receiving does not mean approving of injustice, excusing harm, or calling pain good. We may need to grieve, resist something wrong, or take decisive action. But we can do those things with God rather than spending our strength insisting that the present must first become something else.

Sometimes receiving begins quietly: acknowledge what is here, notice what it stirs in you, and bring both into God's presence. Before we try to do something for Him, we can be received by Him. This moment does not have to be impressive or comfortable to become a place of fellowship and formation.

The present is not an obstacle to life with God. We can spend so much attention resisting what is happening that we miss the place where God is already available to us. Receiving the moment does not make pain good or wrongdoing acceptable. It means we do not have to wait for circumstances to improve before we can listen, trust, grieve honestly, or respond faithfully.

BECOME · FAITHFULNESS IN THE PRESENT

Take the Next Right Step. Repeat.

Then I take the next right step.

Not the next twenty steps. Not the step that guarantees the outcome I want. Not the step that removes every uncertainty.

Just the next faithful step available to me.

Releasing control and receiving the moment prepare us to act differently. Instead of letting fear, the need for approval, or the demand for certainty decide our response, we can consider what love, truth, and faithfulness require in the situation actually before us.

The next right step might mean listening before defending ourselves, speaking honestly without trying to manage another person's reaction, apologizing, setting a necessary boundary, or patiently completing an ordinary responsibility. It may be difficult, and it may not produce the result we hoped for. Its value lies in responding faithfully, not in guaranteeing an outcome.

This is where trust becomes visible. We cannot always choose our circumstances, but we can increasingly learn to choose how we participate with God within them.

And then I repeat the process.

That last part matters.

A single moment of surrender does not instantly undo years of familiar responses. The same fears and desires may surface again tomorrow. Repeating the pattern is not a sign that we have failed; it is how a different way of living becomes familiar.

With practice we begin to notice sooner, release our demands more readily, and respond with greater freedom. The aim is not perfect performance. It is becoming a person who naturally turns toward God in the ordinary movement of life, rather than treating His presence as something reserved for exceptional moments.

We repeat because formation happens through living. Tomorrow will bring another person, another inconvenience, another disappointment, another opportunity, another fear, another decision, or another ordinary moment in which the person we have become will show up.

And when that happens, we practice again.

Here is what the pattern looks like when we carry it into an ordinary day:

01 · Release control.

Recognize where you are insisting on a particular outcome. Bring your hopes and fears to God, do what is yours to do, and surrender the demand to manage what happens next.

02 · Receive the moment.

Stop fighting the fact that this is the moment in front of you. Pay attention to what is happening, what it reveals in you, and where God is inviting you to be present with Him.

03 · Take the next right step.

Respond to what love, truth, and faithfulness require now. You do not need the whole plan or a guaranteed result—only the next step you can take with God.

04 · Repeat.

Return to this way of living when the next interruption, fear, or opportunity arrives. Formation grows through repeated trust in ordinary moments, not through one perfect response.

When someone changes your plans

You had planned a quiet evening, but your spouse tells you that people are coming over. You feel tension rise. Your first thought is, “My time doesn’t matter. Everyone assumes I’ll just go along.” You want to snap or withdraw.

Release control.

You notice the demand underneath your reaction: “The evening has to go as I planned before I can be okay.” You bring it honestly to God: “I wanted some rest, and I’m frustrated. Help me release the demand that everything must happen my way.” Your need for rest still matters; you are making room to respond without letting frustration lead.

Receive the moment.

The conversation in front of you becomes the place to be with God. You acknowledge your disappointment and listen to what your spouse actually says. Perhaps there is something about the situation you haven’t understood.

Take the next right step.

You say, “I was counting on a quiet evening, and I wish we’d talked before making plans. Tell me what you have in mind so we can work out tonight together.” You speak truthfully, listen, and make a decision. The next step might involve welcoming the guests, adjusting the plans, or agreeing on a boundary.

Repeat.

Later, while preparing for the visit, resentment rises again. You notice it and return to God. You don’t have to restart the whole evening or pretend the feeling disappeared. You release your demand again and choose how to respond to the next moment.

The practical result: The evening may still be tiring. Your spouse may not respond as you hoped. But frustration has less control over what you say and do. You are learning to bring your will, words, and actions into alignment with God in an ordinary situation.

JAMES 1:22 · WEB
“But be doers of the word, and not only hearers, deluding your own selves.”

James is not calling us to frantic religious activity. He is reminding us that truth becomes meaningful in the way we live it. The next step is often smaller than the outcome we want: a truthful conversation, a patient response, or a necessary boundary. We do not obey to force results; we respond because we are learning to walk with God.

BECOME · THE SLOW WORK OF FORMATION

Slowly, Something Begins to Happen

What once required enormous effort becomes a little more natural. Situations that once controlled us begin losing some of their power. We notice ourselves sooner. We surrender more readily. We recover from our reactions faster. We become less dependent on circumstances being arranged exactly as we want them.

Our relationships begin changing because we are changing.

2 CORINTHIANS 3:18 · WEB
“But we all, with unveiled face seeing the glory of the Lord as in a mirror, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, even as from the Lord, the Spirit.”

Sometimes the change is in how we return

A familiar fear may still arise. We may still feel the urge to defend ourselves or regain control. Yet something has changed when we notice sooner, hold the feeling more lightly, or turn toward God before it directs the entire encounter. Those quiet returns are not a failure to be transformed. They are often evidence that a different way of living is taking root.

When an unexpected bill arrives

You open a bill you were not expecting. Money is already stretched, and you cannot see how you will cover it. Your chest tightens, and your thoughts rush ahead: “We can’t handle this. I should have prevented it. What happens if something else goes wrong?” This is a place to practice the pattern we are learning.

Release control. You acknowledge the fear and your demand to have the whole problem solved immediately. You bring both to God: “I’m afraid, and I don’t know how we will cover this. Help me release the demand to know how everything will work out before I can take a step with You.”

Receive the moment. You look at what is actually in front of you: a bill that needs attention. The cost is real, but the worst future you are imagining has not happened. You can acknowledge what you do not know and meet this moment with God.

Take the next right step. You check the amount and due date, look at what money is available, and call to ask about payment options. You may need to talk honestly with your spouse or someone you trust. You take the action available today, even while the full solution remains unclear.

Repeat. Later that evening, worry rises again. You notice it without condemning yourself and return to God. You release the demand for certainty, receive the moment you are in, and consider whether there is another practical step to take or whether today’s work is done.

The bill may still be unpaid. What begins to change is how you carry it: you are learning to face a real responsibility with God, without letting fear decide every thought and action.

Our bodies learn different responses. Our wills become stronger in different directions. Our desires begin changing. The various parts of our lives become increasingly integrated around the life we are actually living with God.

And eventually something appears that cannot be manufactured for very long: fruit.

BECOME · THE FRUIT OF A RESTORED LIFE

What Grows from Living With God

Over time, a life lived with God begins to bear a different kind of fruit. Love becomes less conditional. Peace becomes less dependent on circumstances. Patience begins to replace urgency, and kindness becomes possible even when we have nothing to gain. These changes are not proof that we have mastered the process; they are signs of a different source of life.

GALATIANS 5:22–23 · WEB
“But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, and self-control. Against such things there is no law.”

Paul calls these qualities the fruit of the Spirit, not the accomplishments of a disciplined personality. A branch does not make itself fruitful through effort alone. It bears fruit by remaining connected to the life of the vine. Our part is not to manufacture the appearance of Christlikeness, but to live with Christ in ways that allow His life to shape ours.

Fruit becomes visible in relationships

We may notice that we listen before defending ourselves, remain steady when plans change, or offer grace where we once demanded recognition. The people around us experience the difference. This is why becoming is never merely private: what God reforms within us begins to change what flows from us.

And that brings us to the next movement. We do not need to prove ourselves useful to God. As we learn to live with Him, we become more available to participate in what He is already doing.

NOTICE WHERE GOD IS WORKING

A Restored Soul Becomes Available

God is not restoring our souls simply so that we can become healthier versions of ourselves. The freedom we discover as we release control and learn to live with Him makes us increasingly available to others.

We do not have to invent a grand assignment or prove that our lives are useful. We can begin by noticing the people, needs, and opportunities already present. An interruption may become an invitation to listen. A difficult relationship may become an opportunity to love without controlling the outcome.

EPHESIANS 2:10 · WEB
“For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared before that we would walk in them.”

Join is not another performance to master. It is what becomes possible when we are less occupied with protecting ourselves, securing recognition, and arranging life around our own needs.

Jesus repeatedly noticed people others passed by. Before He offered an answer or acted, He saw a person. Join begins with that kind of attention: not looking for something impressive to accomplish, but becoming present enough to recognize an invitation already before us.

JOIN · BECOME AVAILABLE

Notice, Then Respond

Noticing a need and responding to it are different things. We may recognize someone's distress without knowing whether they need practical help, a listening ear, or simply space. Availability begins by listening before deciding what role we should play.

Becoming available does not mean every problem is ours to solve. We can recognize someone's need and offer help without assuming responsibility for their choices or the outcome. Sometimes the next right step is practical help; sometimes it is a conversation, a prayer, or simply being present.

Helping is not taking over

Helping

Offer attention, support, and truth while honoring freedom.

Taking over

Assume responsibility for another person’s decisions and results.

GALATIANS 6:2 · WEB
“Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ.”

Paul calls us to bear one another's burdens, not to live another person's life for them. The distinction is not an excuse to withdraw; it helps us offer support that strengthens rather than replaces the person we hope to serve.

JOIN · SERVE AND WALK WITH PEOPLE

Love Without Control

Serving is not simply completing tasks for God. It is making the love we have received available to someone else. We may offer time, attention, encouragement, practical assistance, or the courage to speak truth.

1 CORINTHIANS 13:4–5 · WEB
“Love is patient and is kind. Love doesn’t envy. Love doesn’t brag, is not proud, doesn’t behave itself inappropriately, doesn’t seek its own way.”

Walking with people requires patience. Their choices may not match our expectations and their growth may not happen on our timetable. Love can remain present without trying to force another person's transformation.

Love may call us to tell an unwelcome truth, establish a boundary, or stay near someone who is struggling. None of these requires us to obtain agreement. We can remain faithful in how we love even when another person's decisions remain their own.

JOIN · GIVE WHAT YOU HAVE RECEIVED

Live Useful, Not Indispensable

What God is forming within us is not meant to remain private. Peace becomes a steady presence for someone who is anxious. Patience makes room for another person's struggle. Grace interrupts blame. Our willingness to listen helps someone feel seen.

Our contribution need not look like anyone else's. A person who listens well, a person who notices practical needs, and a person who offers hard-won wisdom may each participate in God's work in a distinct way. The question is not whose gift appears greatest, but whether what we have received is available to love others.

What we have to give

Presence. Attentive companionship.

Experience. Wisdom shaped by what we have received.

Action. Practical help offered freely.

1 PETER 4:10 · WEB
“As each has received a gift, employ it in serving one another, as good managers of the grace of God in its various forms.”

Peter's language is stewardship: the gift was received before it was offered. That frees us from comparing our contribution with another's or measuring its worth by recognition. We give because we have received.

JOIN · THE JOURNEY CONTINUES

Remain, and Live From What You Have Received

Joining God's work is not the final achievement of a spiritual program. It is the ordinary expression of a life increasingly rooted in Him. We notice, listen, offer what we have, and sometimes discover that our own expectations and desire for control have followed us into the very work we hoped to do for others.

JOHN 15:5 · WEB
“I am the vine. You are the branches. He who remains in me, and I in him, the same bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing.”

Jesus does not tell the branches to become indispensable. He invites them to remain. Fruit is the consequence of connection, not the reward for constant activity.

When service becomes striving, the invitation is not to work harder at being useful. It is to return to the One from whom life flows. The same ordinary pattern we have practiced remains available: release control, receive the moment, take the next right step, and repeat. We do not need to master it before we can begin living it.

Awaken helps us notice. See Clearly helps us recognize what is true. Become teaches us to live with God in the present. Join makes that life available to others. These are not four separate achievements, but movements of one continuing relationship—a life through which His kingdom becomes visible here and now.

The journey continues in the life you are living.

Remain with Him. Pay attention. Give what you have received.
