AN INVITATION TO LIFE WITH GOD

A Different Way of Living With God

Much of the Christian life is described in terms of what we believe and what we do for God. But beneath all of that is an invitation to share our lives with Him.

Reforming the Soul is an invitation to explore that possibility.

Throughout Scripture, we discover a God who desires more than our obedience, service, or religious devotion. He desires relationship. From the beginning, humanity was created for life in His presence—not merely to believe in Him, but to walk with Him.

And that relationship was never intended to be separate from ordinary life.

This journey explores what it means to recover that way of living. It is not another set of religious obligations, but a journey in which life with God reaches beyond what we know and begins to shape who we are becoming.

Along the way, we will learn to recognize what has shaped us, see ourselves and God more clearly, live with Him in the present, and participate in what He is doing in the world around us.

There is no spiritual performance to achieve here, and no ideal version of yourself you must become before beginning.

The invitation is simply to discover what life with God can actually become.

The Person We've Become

Most of us begin the journey of spiritual formation already deeply formed. Long before we gave much thought to the person we were becoming, our families, relationships, successes, failures and experiences with God were shaping us.

Much of that formation happened without our awareness.

We learned to protect ourselves before we knew we were protecting ourselves. Perhaps keeping the peace was the safest way to remain close to someone, or being capable was the surest way to earn their respect. Through experiences of conflict, disappointment, acceptance and rejection, we developed expectations about other people and ways of navigating life that we rarely stopped to examine.

Eventually, these responses became so familiar that they simply felt like us.

Then we came to God carrying all of it.

We may have learned what Christians believe and how Christians are supposed to behave. We may have learned Scripture, attended church, prayed, served, worshiped, and genuinely desired to follow Jesus. Yet underneath all of that, the person who had been formed through years of living was still there.

That is where Reforming the Soul begins.

It does not begin by asking us to try harder to behave like better Christians. It begins by helping us recognize the person from whom our behavior is already coming.

A MOMENT TO NOTICE

What has shaped you?

Much of our formation happened without our awareness. Tap an experience to explore how it can shape us. Notice what resonates with your own story; there is no right answer.

Our earliest surroundings often teach us what feels normal, safe, and expected. We may carry those lessons into adulthood without realizing where they began.

Relationships teach us what closeness, trust, acceptance and rejection feel like. We may carry those experiences into new relationships, expecting things of people who had nothing to do with the circumstances in which those expectations were formed.

Success can teach us to associate worth with achievement. Failure can make us hesitant to try again. Neither experience has to define us, but both can influence what we believe about ourselves.

Pain can leave us expecting another loss, protecting ourselves before anything has happened. Sometimes a response that once helped us endure a difficult season continues long after that season has passed.

When belonging seems to depend on meeting someone else's expectations, we may learn to become whatever is needed of us. Over time, the desire to be accepted can become so intertwined with our choices that we no longer recognize how much of ourselves we are withholding.

Not every formative experience fits a category. A particular season, responsibility, change, or unexpected moment may have influenced the way you see yourself, others, or God.

AWAKEN

We Begin by Waking Up

The first movement is Awaken, because we cannot intentionally surrender something we cannot see.

Imagine someone says something that makes you feel dismissed. Before you have had time to think about it, something happens inside you. Maybe you become defensive. Maybe you withdraw. Maybe you feel an immediate need to explain yourself. Perhaps you become angry or spend the rest of the day replaying the conversation.

Normally, we focus on the other person.

They shouldn't have said that.

Awaken asks us to notice something else.

What just happened in me?

That is a very different question.

We are not immediately deciding whether our response was right or wrong. We are not condemning ourselves for having it. We are simply learning to pay attention.

Why did that bother me so much? Why did I feel threatened? Why was it so important that I be understood? Why do I respond this way whenever someone questions me? Why does this particular situation keep producing the same reaction?

Sometimes the first thing we notice is the conversation already going on within us: “They don't respect me,” “I need to explain,” or “I can't let this happen again.” We may also notice our shoulders tighten or our voice change before we have decided what to say. These are small opportunities to become aware of what is happening within us while the moment is still unfolding.

At first, we may recognize these things long after they happen. Then we begin catching them sooner. Eventually, we sometimes recognize them while they are happening.

That growing awareness matters because we are beginning to recognize the person from whom our responses come, rather than noticing only the responses themselves.

Much of what we call our personality may reflect ways we learned to navigate life. Our soul learned to protect us from rejection, disappointment, uncertainty, and pain. Those responses often served a purpose, but over time they could become automatic ways of interpreting and responding to the world.

Some of the ways we learned to protect ourselves may once have helped us navigate something difficult. We are not trying to eliminate the soul or condemn the person we have become. We are learning to bring those places within us into harmony with the Spirit, allowing God to become the source of the security we have tried to provide for ourselves.

We are learning to catch ourselves being ourselves.

And eventually we can say, “Something is happening in me, and I can see it.”

That is awakening.

But seeing what is happening does not necessarily tell us why it is happening. For that, we have to look deeper.

JAMES 1:19–20 · WEB
“So, then, my beloved brothers, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger; for the anger of man doesn't produce the righteousness of God.”

James describes more than a way of restraining anger. Learning to listen before we react gives us room to recognize what is rising within us and become available to God before a familiar response takes over.

AWAKEN · PRACTICING ATTENTION

Notice Without Needing to Explain

When something unsettles us, we usually turn our attention outward. We replay what someone said, what should have happened, or what needs to change. Sometimes those concerns are legitimate. But there is another part of the moment we can learn to notice: what is happening within us while we respond.

Jesus does not invite us to become suspicious of everything we feel or spend our lives analyzing ourselves. We can acknowledge a reaction without condemning ourselves for having it or immediately trying to justify it. What we are beginning to see gives us something real to bring into our relationship with God.

PSALM 139:23–24 · WEB
“Search me, God, and know my heart. Try me, and know my thoughts. See if there is any wicked way in me, and lead me in the everlasting way.”

David invites God to search the places within him that he cannot fully see himself. He does not need to understand everything about his own heart before entrusting it to the One who knows him.

Bring an ordinary moment into view

Recall a recent moment that stayed with you. Something interrupted your plans, disappointed you, pleased you, or brought a stronger reaction than you expected.

Notice what happened and how you responded. There is no need to explain or judge your reaction.

For example: “Someone corrected me, and I immediately began explaining myself. I noticed tension and a strong need to be understood.”

What happened, and what did you notice?

Take a moment to consider this quietly with God, then continue when you are ready.

“Lord, here is what I noticed. I do not need to understand it all before I bring it to You.”

PART I · SEE YOURSELF CLEARLY

Then We Learn to See Clearly

Someone questions a decision you have made, and suddenly the conversation is no longer about the decision. You feel the need to establish that you knew what you were doing, to explain why you made the choice, or to defend your ability to make it. Somewhere in the exchange, what you hear becomes more significant than what was actually said.

Perhaps being questioned has come to mean being doubted. Perhaps being wrong has become associated with losing the respect of someone whose opinion matters to you. Those associations can become so deeply ingrained that we experience them as reality rather than as conclusions we have drawn from our experiences.

We respond not only to what is happening, but to what we have come to believe it means.

Awaken brings those responses into view. See Clearly invites us to look at the understanding of life from which they come. We begin to discover how much of what feels true to us has been shaped by the life we have already lived.

ROMANS 12:2 · WEB
“Don’t be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind, so that you may prove what is the good, well-pleasing, and perfect will of God.”

The renewing of our minds involves more than acquiring new information about God. It reaches into the convictions we have been living from, some of which we may never have put into words. God is not asking us to deny what we have experienced; He is inviting us to see it in the light of what is true.

Over time, the ways we learned to navigate life can become part of how we understand ourselves. The person who learned to anticipate rejection may come to believe they are someone people inevitably leave. The person who learned to establish their worth through achievement may have difficulty imagining they could be loved apart from what they accomplish.

There is a distinction between the person we have been formed to be and the life we have been given in Christ. We can acknowledge the history behind our responses without allowing that history to determine our identity. As we begin to see ourselves and God more clearly, we discover that the things we have long believed about ourselves do not have to govern the life we share with Him.

PART I · SEE YOURSELF CLEARLY · THE INNER PATH

What We See Shapes What We Become

What we see shapes what we believe. What we believe shapes what we expect. What we expect influences what we desire. What we desire influences what we intend.

I encounter an experience and give it meaning, often without being aware that I am doing so. What I see is influenced by what I have already lived through.

The meaning I give that experience may reinforce a conviction I already carry about myself, others or God.

What I believe shapes what I anticipate will happen next, and I begin preparing for that possibility.

What I expect awakens a desire: to feel secure, to be accepted, to remain in control, or to avoid experiencing a familiar hurt.

My desire begins directing my will toward what I want to obtain or what I want to prevent.

My intention becomes visible in a decision: what I say, what I withhold, how I respond, or what I do.

As choices are repeated, they become familiar patterns. What began as a way of seeing becomes a way of living.

PROVERBS 4:23 · WEB
“Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of life.”

When Scripture speaks of the heart, it is speaking about more than our emotions. It is speaking about the inner life from which our desires, judgments and choices emerge. What becomes visible in our behavior may already have been taking shape within us for some time.

This is why changing behavior alone does not necessarily change the person from whom it comes.

Maybe what appears to be anger is actually self-protection. Perhaps what looks like ambition is being fueled by a need for approval. What looks like independence may actually reveal how difficult it is for us to trust someone else.

We may become quite capable of responding appropriately while still carrying the resentment, fear or need for approval that produced the original response.

When a belief becomes an identity

We do not only form opinions about events. Over time we also form a story about ourselves: I am the responsible one. I am the one people leave. I have to be useful to be wanted. I must never make a mistake. A story repeated often enough can begin to feel like a fact, even when it was first learned in a particular season of life.

If my acceptance depends on being right, correction feels threatening. If my worth depends on being needed, another person's independence may feel like rejection. If I have learned to expect disappointment, I may withdraw before someone has had the opportunity to love me well. These are patterns worth recognizing, not identities to assign to ourselves or others.

EPHESIANS 2:8–10 · WEB
“For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, that no one would boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works”

Paul places our identity as God's workmanship before the good works that follow. Our choices matter, but they do not purchase our place with Him. Grace changes the place from which we live: we learn to respond from the life we have received rather than trying to earn it.

Guilt, shame, and the invitation to return

Guilt can draw our attention to something we have done that needs to be acknowledged and repaired. Shame can take that experience further, persuading us that what we did tells the whole truth about who we are. We can take responsibility for our actions without building our identity around our failures.

After their disobedience, Adam and Eve hide from God. Their choices have consequences, but hiding also changes the way they relate to His presence. We can recognize something of ourselves in that moment: the impulse to withdraw from the One who invites us to bring what is wrong into the light.

PART I · SEE YOURSELF CLEARLY

Look Beneath Your Response

Look beneath an ordinary moment

Recall a recent situation that brought a reaction you would like to understand.

What did you take that moment to mean? Perhaps you assumed something about yourself, the other person, or what would happen next. You do not have to uncover a hidden motive. Simply notice the meaning you gave the experience.

How This Happens in Everyday Life

Let’s look at how an ordinary situation can reveal what is happening beneath our response.

When Someone Criticizes You

Someone points out a problem with work I put effort into.

They think I am not good enough.

Nothing I do will satisfy them.

I want to feel accepted and appreciated.

I will protect myself from feeling inadequate.

I defend myself or dismiss what they say.

If repeated, I become defensive even when someone is trying to help.

What might I not be seeing? The criticism may be intended to help, rather than to judge my worth.

When Your Plans Are Interrupted

My plans are unexpectedly interrupted by someone’s request.

They do not respect my time.

People will keep taking advantage of me.

I want to stay in control of my day.

I will protect my plans from interference.

I respond with irritation or refuse to help.

If repeated, I become less available to others and more controlling.

What might I not be seeing? The interruption may be an opportunity to care for someone, or simply an inconvenience that does not require me to defend myself.

What was I trying to protect, and what might God be inviting me to see differently?

What were you taking to be true?

Take a moment to consider this quietly with God, then continue when you are ready.

2 CORINTHIANS 10:5 · WEB
“bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ”

Paul is addressing arguments raised against the knowledge of God. His words are not an invitation to examine every passing thought, but they remind us that even a deeply held conviction can be brought before Christ and tested against what is true.

There is a difference between what happened, what we understood it to mean, and what we actually know. The more familiar an interpretation becomes, the more difficult it can be to distinguish it from the experience itself.

Imagine sending a message to a friend and receiving no reply. The fact is that you have not heard back. The conclusion may be that you no longer matter to them. That conclusion can feel entirely true, but it is not the only possible explanation. We can bring both the experience and our interpretation before God without pretending either that the hurt is unreal or that our conclusion is certain.

Independence can look like strength

Being capable, responsible and dependable are not problems in themselves. But when we have learned to rely entirely on ourselves, dependence may feel like a loss of strength. We can bring that same independence into prayer, asking God to bless what we have already decided rather than making room for Him as we decide.

JOHN 15:4–5 · WEB
“Remain in me, and I in you. As the branch can’t bear fruit by itself unless it remains in the vine, so neither can you, unless you remain in me.”

Jesus describes fruitfulness as the result of remaining in Him. A branch receives its life from the vine; it does not become fruitful by learning to live independently of it. Formation is not about becoming a self-sufficient Christian, but learning to live from the life of Christ.

That is a deeper kind of clarity: recognizing not only what I do when I am afraid, but also the picture of life I have trusted. Am I trying to secure myself through control, approval, and performance, or learning to receive my life with God?

PART II · SEE GOD CLEARLY

SEE GOD CLEARLY

We have begun examining the person we have become. Now we turn toward the One we are learning to trust. What we believe about God shapes what we expect from Him, and those expectations influence how we live with Him.

What Do I Actually Believe About God?

Eventually, looking beneath our responses brings us to an even deeper question: What do I actually believe about God? Not simply what have I been taught about God, but what do I expect from Him when life is not going the way I want?

What do I expect from Him when I am disappointed? Do I believe He is good when He allows something I desperately wanted to avoid? Do I experience Him as a Father who wants me, or as someone constantly evaluating my performance?

Do I believe His acceptance must somehow be maintained through my behavior? Can I trust Him when I cannot understand what He is doing?

Knowing what Christians say about God is not necessarily the same as living from what we believe about Him.

These are not questions for judging ourselves. They help us recognize the expectations we actually bring into our relationship with God.

JOHN 14:9 · WEB
“He who has seen me has seen the Father.”

Jesus makes an extraordinary claim: the Father is not someone whose character we must guess at from our circumstances. When we look at Jesus, we see the Father. He is not persuading a reluctant Father to love us; He is revealing the Father who sent Him.

Some of us confess that God loves us while approaching Him as though His welcome depends on how well we have performed. Others believe He is sovereign but experience His authority primarily as control. The difference between what we profess and what we expect gives us something worth exploring with Him.

How our picture of God is formed

Our experience of authority can influence the way we imagine God. A distant parent, an unpredictable leader, a demanding religious environment or a season of unanswered prayer may shape what we expect from Him. These experiences deserve honesty, but none is sufficient to define the Father.

We might approach Him as disappointed before we have spoken, controlling before we have listened, or merely tolerating us until we improve. Such assumptions shape prayer and surrender. It is difficult to release control to someone we secretly believe is unkind. It is difficult to receive love while assuming every gift creates a debt.

LUKE 15:20 · WEB
“But while he was still far off, his father saw him, and was moved with compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.”

In Jesus' parable, the son prepares a speech before returning home. His father runs to meet him before he has a chance to make things right. The story does not erase the consequences of his choices; it reveals a father whose love is already moving toward restoration.

PART II · SEE GOD CLEARLY · JESUS AS OUR LENS

Learning to See God as He Is

As we begin to recognize the picture of God we carry, we have somewhere to bring it. Jesus reveals the Father, giving us a way to examine what we have come to believe about Him.

We may have learned to approach God as distant, disappointed, controlling, unpredictable, or merely tolerating us. Those expectations can shape how we pray, how we respond to difficulty, and whether we trust Him with what matters most.

Jesus does not simply give us another set of instructions. He reveals the Father's character. We can bring our inherited assumptions into the light of His life, His words, and the way He receives people.

Jesus is not the exception to what God is like. He is the revelation of what God is like.

As we come to know the Father Jesus reveals, the need to earn His acceptance begins to lose its hold. Dependence becomes possible where we once relied on ourselves, and surrender becomes an expression of trust rather than fear.

HEBREWS 1:3 · WEB
“His Son is the radiance of his glory, the very image of his substance”

Jesus makes the Father's character visible in the way He receives people: the fearful, the ashamed, the overlooked and those who have failed. His compassion does not require us to pretend sin is harmless, and His truth does not require us to imagine that the Father is less loving than the Son.

Seeing God clearly does not mean we can explain every disappointment. When our circumstances seem to contradict His goodness, we can return to the One who reveals His heart.

Jesus reveals more than a reassuring idea

When Jesus touches those considered unclean, eats with people others reject, challenges religious performance and forgives those who have failed, He is not making an exception to the Father's character. He is making it known. His mercy meets people in truth and invites them into life.

LUKE 10:22 · WEB
“No one knows who the Son is, except the Father, and who the Father is, except the Son, and he to whomever the Son desires to reveal him.”

Jesus invites us to know the Father He knows. We do not have to build our understanding of God from the authority figures we have experienced or the disappointments we have endured. We can bring those impressions to Jesus and allow what He reveals to reshape what we expect from the Father.

In John 14–17, Jesus does more than reveal His own relationship with the Father. Through the Spirit, He invites us to share in that life and to learn the same attentiveness and trust we see in Him.

Trust does not require an explanation for everything

There will be experiences we cannot explain. Scripture gives us room to grieve, question and wait. Knowing the character of the One with whom we walk allows us to remain in relationship even when our questions remain unanswered.

Bring your understanding to Jesus

Think of an expectation you carry about yourself or God, especially when something is difficult.

Hold that understanding alongside what Jesus reveals about the Father. Is there an assumption you can now hold more lightly, or a truth you can begin to trust? You do not need to force an answer. Bring what remains uncertain into conversation with Him.

PART II · SEE GOD CLEARLY · WHAT IS TRUE

Understanding Where It Came From

Awaken made it possible to say, “I know what I did.” See Clearly invites us to go further: “I am starting to understand where that came from, and I am learning what is actually true.”

Recognizing that anger has its roots in fear, or that our need to control grew out of an expectation of abandonment, can change how we understand ourselves. But the purpose of that understanding is not to become experts at explaining ourselves. It is to bring what we are discovering into our life with God.

We are learning to see ourselves and God truthfully, so that our relationship with Him can be lived from trust rather than fear, performance, or self-protection.

Trust precedes understanding.

But even clear understanding is not the same as becoming the kind of person who naturally lives from what is true.

From an inherited picture to a living relationship

There is a difference between agreeing that God is good and entrusting something we deeply fear to Him. There is a difference between knowing we are accepted in Christ and remaining with Him when failure makes us want to hide. Truth begins to move from something we affirm into something we live.

EPHESIANS 1:17–18 · WEB
“…that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give to you a spirit of wisdom and revelation in the knowledge of him, having the eyes of your hearts enlightened, that you may know what is the hope of his calling…”

Paul is praying for people who already believe. He wants them to see more clearly what God has given them. We may know the language of faith while still approaching God with expectations of rejection or disappointment. As the eyes of our hearts are opened, we can begin to live from the welcome we have received.

Suppose you are waiting for news that matters deeply to you. Trust may begin simply by telling God what you hope for, admitting what you fear, and choosing to remain with Him while you wait. You do not have to know what He will do before you can entrust yourself to Him.

Seeing Is Not Yet Living

We have begun to recognize how our interpretations, beliefs, and expectations shape the way we respond. We have also looked again at the character of God through Jesus, rather than allowing fear or disappointment to define Him for us.

Seeing clearly changes the foundation from which we can live with God. Yet a new understanding does not immediately replace years of familiar responses. We begin learning what it means to live from what is true in the ordinary moments in which those responses arise.

Trust precedes understanding.

We can begin living from what is true before we understand everything that will follow.

BECOME · LIVING WITH GOD

Learning to Live From Trust

We may understand something clearly while sitting quietly with God and find ourselves reacting in an old familiar way a few hours later. Our lives are formed in those ordinary moments when something interrupts us, disappoints us or asks more of us than we expected. They are not interruptions to formation; they are where it becomes a way of living.

Jesus lived in continual relationship with the Father, listening and responding in the circumstances before Him. The life we are learning is not a collection of correct conclusions or extraordinary spiritual experiences. It is a life of participation with God in the moments we already have.

JOHN 5:19 · WEB
“Most certainly, I tell you, the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing. For whatever things he does, these the Son also does likewise.”

Jesus reveals what it means to live and act with the Father rather than independently of Him. Through the Spirit, we are invited to learn that way of living.

That kind of life takes practice. We learn to loosen our grip on outcomes, meet the present with God, respond faithfully, and return when we lose our way. The pattern is simple enough to remember, yet deep enough to reshape the way we live.

01 · Release control
02 · Receive the moment
03 · Take the next right step
04 · Repeat

These are not four tasks to perform perfectly. They are a way of returning to relationship with God in the life we are actually living.

Before exploring this rhythm, it is worth recognizing the relationship in which it takes place. God is already present with us, and we can learn to listen to Him.

BECOME · PRESENCE

Recognizing His Presence

It is possible to spend time in prayer and then return to the rest of our day as though our time with God were over. We may have become so accustomed to thinking of His presence in terms of particular activities that we overlook the One who is already with us.

Prayer, worship and quiet moments matter, but they do not contain our relationship with God. Preparing a meal, working, waiting in traffic and listening to someone tell us about their day can all become part of the life we share with Him.

JOHN 14:23 · WEB
“If a man loves me, he will keep my word. My Father will love him, and we will come to him, and make our home with him.”

Jesus speaks of God making His home with us. His presence is not something we create through our attention, nor is it dependent on our ability to feel it. We are learning to recognize and receive what is already true.

Imagine standing at the kitchen sink with a mind full of unfinished responsibilities. Nothing about the moment has to change. You can turn your attention toward God and say, “You are here with me, even in this.” Then continue with what is before you, aware that you are not doing it alone.

We will forget, become distracted, and return again. That is how attention becomes familiar. We are not trying to maintain a particular feeling; we are learning to share the life we already have with Him.

BECOME · LISTENING

Learning to Listen

As we become more aware of God's presence, the way we pray can begin to change. Instead of bringing Him only our requests or our understanding of what is happening, we can make room for Him to help us see what we have not yet seen.

Perhaps a conversation has left us frustrated. Rather than immediately deciding what to say or how to fix it, we can bring the experience to God. “Father, what am I not seeing here?” We can allow a little space for Him to bring something to our attention, without demanding an immediate answer.

JOHN 14:26 · WEB
“But the Counselor, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you all things, and will remind you of all that I said to you.”

Jesus describes the Spirit as present and active, teaching and reminding us of what He has said. Listening is part of learning to participate in that relationship.

Sometimes a passage of Scripture comes to mind. Sometimes we begin to see another person differently or recognize a desire that has been directing us. We need not assume every thought is from God; we can consider what we notice in the light of Scripture and the character of Jesus, and seek wise counsel when a decision matters. Listening does not require us to manufacture certainty.

Even if we do not receive a clear answer, we can remain with Him. We may already know something love and truth require of us in the moment. Listening does not need to become another thing we measure ourselves by.

BECOME · RELEASE CONTROL

Release Control.

Release control

I release my demand that this situation must turn out the way I want before I can be okay.

Recognizing our need for control is not the same as surrendering it. We may understand the fear beneath our response and still find ourselves insisting that things unfold a certain way. Releasing control means letting go of the demand that our preferred outcome must happen before we can trust God.

Surrender is not the abandonment of our will or our responsibility. We can name what we want, make decisions and act with courage, while bringing our will into relationship with God's without assuming we already know how everything should turn out.

Control can disguise itself as responsibility. We may call it careful planning, protecting someone, or making sure things go well. But when our peace depends on a particular outcome, responsibility has become a demand. We are trying to carry what belongs to God.

PROVERBS 3:5–6 · WEB
“Trust in Yahweh with all your heart, and don’t lean on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make your paths straight.”

Releasing control is not refusing to plan or neglecting what is entrusted to us. We still make decisions, tell the truth, set boundaries, and do what love requires. What we release is the insistence that our obedience must produce the result we prefer. We can tell God what we hope for and what we fear without demanding that He follow our timetable.

I am responsible for my response, not for controlling the outcome.

Trust precedes understanding. We can take the next faithful step even when we cannot see how the story will unfold.

Responsibility has a boundary. We can be honest with another person, but we cannot guarantee their response. We can act faithfully today without securing tomorrow's outcome. Recognizing that distinction allows us to do what is ours without trying to carry what is not.

BECOME · RECEIVE THE MOMENT

Receive the Moment.

Receive the moment

I can meet the life in front of me with God, rather than waiting for life to become what I wish it were.

So much of our attention can be occupied by something that has already happened or something we fear might happen next. Releasing our demand to control the outcome allows us to become available to the present, the moment in which we can actually listen, respond and live with God.

MATTHEW 6:34 · WEB
“Therefore don’t be anxious for tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Each day’s own evil is sufficient.”

Receiving the moment does not mean pretending that everything happening to us is good. It does not mean becoming passive or refusing to change something that should be changed. It means acknowledging that this is the moment in which I am presently living with God.

If God has allowed this moment to reach me, then this moment cannot prevent Him from forming me through it.

Receiving the moment also means paying attention to what it reveals. An interruption may uncover impatience, disappointment may expose our demand for certainty, and another person's words may reveal how deeply we seek approval. We do not need to hide those discoveries or immediately fix ourselves. We can bring them into our relationship with God.

As we become present to Him, the moment is no longer only a problem to solve. It becomes an opportunity to listen, to see ourselves truthfully, and to respond from trust instead of fear.

That changes the question.

Instead of asking only, How do I get out of this? I can begin asking, What does faithfulness look like here?

Receiving does not mean approving of injustice, excusing harm or calling pain good. We may need to grieve, speak up, seek help or leave an unsafe situation. The invitation is to face what is real with God, rather than believing life with Him must wait until our circumstances change.

We can bring both the circumstance and what it is stirring within us to God. Before we try to do something for Him, we can be received by Him. The moment does not have to be comfortable to become a place of life with Him.

The present is not an obstacle to life with God. We do not have to resolve every difficulty before we can listen, trust, grieve honestly or respond faithfully. God is available to us in the life we are living now.

BECOME · FAITHFULNESS IN THE PRESENT

Take the Next Right Step. Repeat.

Then I take the next right step.

Not the next twenty steps. Not the step that guarantees the outcome I want. Not the step that removes every uncertainty.

Just the next faithful step available to me.

Releasing control and receiving the moment create room for a different response. Rather than allowing the need for approval, fear or our insistence on certainty to determine what we do, we can consider what love and truth require of us here.

The next right step might mean listening before defending ourselves, speaking honestly without trying to manage another person's reaction, apologizing, setting a necessary boundary, or patiently completing an ordinary responsibility. It may be difficult, and it may not produce the result we hoped for. Its value lies in responding faithfully, not in guaranteeing an outcome.

This is where trust becomes visible. We cannot always choose our circumstances, but we can increasingly learn to choose how we participate with God within them.

And then I repeat the process.

That last part matters.

One moment of surrender does not erase years of familiar responses. We may release our need to control a conversation in the morning and find it returning that afternoon. What happened earlier has not been undone; we have another opportunity to practice living with God.

With practice we begin to notice sooner, return to God more readily and respond with greater freedom. What once required considerable effort can become a more natural expression of the person we are becoming.

The ordinary movement of life will bring another interruption, disappointment, conversation or decision. We do not need to arrange our lives around opportunities to practice; the opportunities are already there.

And when that happens, we practice again.

Here is what the pattern looks like when we carry it into an ordinary day:

01 · Release control.

Recognize where you are insisting on a particular outcome. Bring your hopes and fears to God, do what is yours to do, and surrender the demand to manage what happens next.

02 · Receive the moment.

Stop fighting the fact that this is the moment in front of you. Pay attention to what is happening, what it reveals in you, and where God is inviting you to be present with Him.

03 · Take the next right step.

Respond to what love, truth, and faithfulness require now. You do not need the whole plan or a guaranteed result—only the next step you can take with God.

04 · Repeat.

Return to this way of living when the next interruption, fear, or opportunity arrives. Formation grows through repeated trust in ordinary moments, not through one perfect response.

When someone changes your plans

You had planned a quiet evening, but your spouse tells you that people are coming over. You feel tension rise. Your first thought is, “My time doesn’t matter. Everyone assumes I’ll just go along.” You want to snap or withdraw.

Release control.

You notice the demand underneath your reaction: “The evening has to go as I planned before I can be okay.” You bring it honestly to God: “I wanted some rest, and I’m frustrated. Help me release the demand that everything must happen my way.” Your need for rest still matters; you are making room to respond without letting frustration lead.

Receive the moment.

The conversation in front of you becomes the place to be with God. You acknowledge your disappointment and listen to what your spouse actually says. Perhaps there is something about the situation you haven’t understood.

Take the next right step.

You say, “I was counting on a quiet evening, and I wish we’d talked before making plans. Tell me what you have in mind so we can work out tonight together.” You speak truthfully, listen, and make a decision. The next step might involve welcoming the guests, adjusting the plans, or agreeing on a boundary.

Repeat.

Later, while preparing for the visit, resentment rises again. You notice it and return to God. You don’t have to restart the whole evening or pretend the feeling disappeared. You release your demand again and choose how to respond to the next moment.

The practical result: The evening may still be tiring. Your spouse may not respond as you hoped. But frustration has less control over what you say and do. You are learning to bring your will, words, and actions into alignment with God in an ordinary situation.
